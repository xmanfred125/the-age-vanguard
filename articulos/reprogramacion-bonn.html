Direct reprogramming: blood cells from donors up to 101 years old rejuvenate their epigenetic age

Published in Aging Cell, October 3, 2026.

A team at the University of Bonn directly converted blood cells from donors up to 101 years old into induced neural stem cells, using only two factors, SOX2 and cMYC, without passing through pluripotency. Epigenetic age measured by the Horvath clock fell to about 13% of the donor’s age in early passages and to about 5% after prolonged culture, with most of the reset happening in about 50 days, even when cell division was blocked. The authors warn that methylation age is a surrogate biomarker, not a measure of the cell’s functional age, and that cMYC is an oncogene, so this is mechanistic work, not a therapy.

What happened

Berg and colleagues (Oliver Brüstle’s lab, Bonn University Hospital) reprogrammed erythroid progenitors from 15 donors, from newborns to 101 years old, directly into neural stem cells.

Why it matters

It shows that epigenetic reset does not need to pass through the pluripotent state or depend on cell division — a key fact for understanding the partial reprogramming already being tested in people with ER-100.

What comes next

Nothing clinical; the next step is to understand whether epigenetic reset equals younger cell function, not just a lower clock reading.

Caution

Methylation age is a surrogate biomarker, not a measure of the cell’s functional age, and cMYC is an oncogene. This is mechanistic work, not a therapy.
