Semaglutide: in more than 10,000 patients, the heart ages 2 to 4 years less according to proteomic clocks

Preprint, September 22, 2026; randomized trial in Nature Communications, June 2026.

An analysis by Novo Nordisk with Harvard collaborators (Vadim Gladyshev) applied organ proteomic clocks to stored blood from 10,052 participants across five randomized semaglutide trials (SELECT, STEP-HFpEF, STEP-HFpEF DM, FLOW and SOUL), with follow-up of up to 156 weeks. The heart’s biological age fell by 2 to 4 years versus placebo in all five trials; kidney, brain, pancreas, lung and multi-organ also fell, by 1 to 4 years, with less consistency. The effect was not fully explained by weight loss, HbA1c or C-reactive protein. Separately, a placebo-controlled randomized trial in Nature Communications (108 adults with HIV-associated lipohypertrophy, 32 weeks) found that semaglutide slowed the pace of epigenetic aging by 9% by DunedinPACE.

What happened

Two independent lines of evidence point the same way: GLP-1 drugs move biological clocks, not just weight.

Why it matters

It is the largest human dataset yet on an already-approved drug and its effect on measured biological age.

What comes next

The large analysis is a preprint without peer review and in sick populations (obesity, diabetes, heart, kidney); an ARPA-H-funded trial will test semaglutide from January in healthy adults over 60, measuring cognition, mobility and senses.

Caution

A younger clock is not proof of rejuvenation or of more years of life; the preprint’s authors say so. Semaglutide is not approved to slow aging.
