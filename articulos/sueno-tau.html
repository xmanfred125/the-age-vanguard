Deep sleep: when tau fragments slow waves, memory does not consolidate

Published in Nature Neuroscience, September 11, 2026.

Researchers at UC Berkeley (Omer Sharon and Matthew Walker, with Washington University in St. Louis) combined high-density EEG during sleep with tau PET. In young adults, slow waves of non-REM sleep travelled across the cortex like a coordinated front; in older adults, the waves travelled shorter distances and appeared in isolation — “lonely waves”. Tau buildup in the frontal cortex was associated with that fragmentation and with worse overnight memory consolidation, and in longitudinal follow-up, those who accumulated tau faster also worsened faster in wave coordination and retention.

What happened

Comparison of young adults (early 20s) with healthy older adults (early 60s–70s), plus a longitudinal sub-analysis over several years.

Why it matters

It connects the molecular pathology of Alzheimer’s with one of its earliest symptoms, memory loss, through a mechanism measurable during sleep.

What comes next

It is not known what changes first, tau or the waves; nor whether improving deep sleep would slow tau.

Caution

Observational; it does not prove causality, nor that every older person with less coordinated waves will develop Alzheimer’s.
